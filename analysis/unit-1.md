#### Modelling justification, 400 to 600 words. Cover your key design decisions: your choice of primary keys, your ON DELETE behaviors, and which rules you chose to enforce in the schema rather than leaving to the application.

The key decision points were mostly in the relationships between entities. I chose to be very strict with my schema design, all fields were required to have a value. This seemed to be the right choice given the sparse nature of the required data. I modeled the minimum, required fields for each entity. I chose the primary keys to be an integer that would be generated by a sequence for each table. The use of a surrogate key will allow for the schema to evolve slightly easier. The choice of key for the viewing table was not made clear in the requirements, so I chose to use a surrogate key for the viewing entity as well. This design also led me to adopt a ON DELETE CASCADE strategy for all foreign key relationships, since all the data is not nullable alternatives would have been to use a ON DELETE SET NULL strategy but that would not have fit. I used a composite key for the join table by convention, this enables the many-to-many relationship between properties and amenities. One of the key decisions I made for the domain was the listing_price field for the properties table. I elected to make this field integer type rather than decimal type, since most house listings do not show the price with cents. And our system has no need to track the cents of the listing price. If something changes we can include the cents as its own field or the total with the fractional amount as a separate field. Originally, I misunderstood the relationship between the entities. My original design had the renter as the owner of the property, not the prospective renter. This put a direct relationship between the renter and property entities. With no relationship between renter and the viewing event. Upon a more careful reading I discovered that the viewing event was the center of the schema. The prospective renter and the property entities on either side of the viewing, such that the viewing event was a many-to-one relationship with the renter and a many-to-one relationship with the property. If we flipped the relationship it would mean that for each event there was mulitple properties, like a listing crawl type of event. I ruled this out. Alternatively if it was many to many with renters it would be more of an open house scenario, which I rulled out as well. The case I modeled was one property and one prospective renter per viewing event. Lastly, I elected to model the property_amenity join table as a composite key by convention, this enables the many-to-many relationship between properties and amenities. 

#### Reflection, 150 to 250 words. Your ERD encodes decisions the requirements left open. Name one decision you made that a different designer could reasonably have made differently, and defend your choice in terms of the read and write patterns the platform will face.

Given the strategy outlined above, the difficulty will be attempting to model open house events. in the case of open house events, where one record in the viewing table would capture the many renters attending. An open house in our model would be represented as a single record in the viewing table for each renter attending. The issue here is that there would need to be a non-unique identifier that would allow us to group renters attending the same open house together, which does not exist in the design. The design I chose was shepherded by the missing join table that would have been required in the documentation. I think for simplicity we could start with private viewings. Although I think that modeling open house viewings now would probably make more sense. In our case here, we should find out what the requirements are for open house viewings and design the model accordingly.
